When a tourism shock spreads across Macao's sectors, which SMEs does a subsidised loan actually save?

The scheme assesses firms one by one. This prototype estimates how a drop in visitors travels through gaming, hotels, restaurants and retail (Diebold–Yilmaz connectedness on DSEC data), simulates each SME profile's cash flow with and without the 4% interest subsidy, and ranks profiles by jobs and health burden protected per pataca.

Transmitters and receivers, with robustness

Read the direction with care. Total connectedness is high in every specification (). Which sector is a net transmitter is not stable: several signs flip between the full sample and the pre-COVID sample. The ranking therefore relies on the impulse responses, not on the transmitter/receiver labels.

Who gains most: the middle, not the weakest

Each bubble is an SME profile. Its tail starts at the chance of surviving without the subsidy and ends where the subsidy takes it; its height is how many jobs it saves per MOP 1m. Long tails high up sit in the middle band. Firms that are safe anyway barely move, yet a flat rate spends most of its money on them.

    Same cost, better rates: a two-tier subsidy

    The scheme is not short of money: about MOP 4.5bn of its MOP 10bn loan cap had been used by 31 March 2026, and applications were extended to 31 December 2026. So the ranking is not for rationing. It tells the government what rate to offer whom. Each row compares a two-tier design with the flat 4% at no higher total cost.

    Does it hold on Macao's own data?

    Three checks against what actually happened in Macao, so the model is not taken on trust.

    1. COVID back-test: estimated only on data before 2020

    Each sector's exposure to a visitor shock was estimated on 2011–2019 only, then applied to the actual 2020–2022 visitor collapse (visitors averaged vs 2019).

    2. Today's structure, measured directly

    Because older data may not describe today's Macao, retail exposure was re-measured from DSEC 2025 visitor spending by category (spending per visitor × arrivals ÷ retail sales of that category).

    3. Forecasting 2025–2026 out of sample

    Method & sources

    Every number in this prototype traces to a row below. Each parameter states its basis: official data, published evidence, or a value derived from them. The sensitivity table shows how much each modelling choice moves the headline results.

    Pipeline

    1. Data. Ten quarterly series from the DSEC Time Series Database, 2010Q1–, retrieved . Year-on-year log growth, winsorised at ±1.0 log points so the 2020–23 collapse and rebound do not dominate.
    2. Connectedness. VAR with lag chosen by BIC; generalised forecast-error variance decomposition (Pesaran–Shin), row-normalised as in Diebold & Yilmaz (2012). Rolling -quarter windows for the time profile.
    3. Shock propagation. The origin's level is fixed to the scenario path; the shocks needed to produce it are solved quarter by quarter from the generalised impulse responses; every other sector's level path follows the network.
    4. Cash-flow simulation. Monthly, 24 months, paths. Revenue = normal revenue × sector path × normal-times sector uncertainty (bootstrapped VAR residuals, COVID quarters excluded). Costs from DSEC 2024 surveys. Each firm starts with its cash buffer and carries a drawn credit line (interest-only). Failure = cash below zero.
    5. Subsidy. The same firm, with the same random draws, is run twice: paying the bank rate, and paying the bank rate minus min(4%, bank rate) while it survives. Expected subsidy cost is the denominator of every per-pataca figure.
    6. Targeting comparison. Untargeted = total jobs saved ÷ total subsidy across all profiles. Targeted = fund profiles in order of jobs per pataca until 25% of that budget is spent.

    Limitations

    • The profile grid is equal-weighted. DSEC publishes establishment counts for retail and restaurants overall, not by retail sub-type, so results are per profile, not per Macao firm.
    • All retail sub-sectors share the aggregate retail cost structure; sector differences come from demand exposure. Restaurant costs come from the restaurant survey.
    • The neighbourhood-restaurant visitor share is pinned to the industry-wide tourism ratio; DSEC does not publish it by restaurant type. Supermarket sales stand in for local demand.
    • Job-loss health effects come from Korean panel data and international meta-analyses, not Macao itself; Macao data enter through mortality and life expectancy. Cash-buffer profiles use US bank-account data because no Macao or Hong Kong distribution is published; halving or doubling them keeps the targeting gain between 2.9x and 3.7x.
    • With no shock, the profile grid fails at a higher rate than Macao's SME loan delinquency ratio (see validation row), mainly because restaurant profiles carry the sector's negative 2024 margin. Results lean pessimistic for restaurants.
    • The model is built to stress-test shocks. Out of sample in 2025–2026 it did not forecast ordinary quarter-to-quarter growth better than a no-change rule (see Validation).
    • The subsidy is modelled as interest relief only. If the subsidy also decides whether a firm borrows at all, the true gains are larger.
    • Restaurant establishment counts jump between 2019 and 2020 in the DSEC survey; only 2024 ratios are used, not counts.
